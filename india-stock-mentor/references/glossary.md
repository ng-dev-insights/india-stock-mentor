# Beginner Glossary

Each entry: **meaning** → **everyday analogy** → **beginner mistake** → **where to see it**. Use the analogy in the user's language; adapt names and examples to their region if helpful. Prerequisites are listed so you can explain the base idea first.

## Level 0 – Money foundations

**Inflation** — Prices rising over time, so ₹100 buys less each year. Analogy: the vada pav that cost ₹10 now costs ₹20. Mistake: thinking money "safe" in a savings account is not losing value. Prereq: none.

**Compounding** — Earning returns on your past returns. Analogy: a mango tree whose mangoes are planted to grow more trees. Worked example: ₹1 lakh at 12% a year becomes about ₹3.1 lakh in 10 years and about ₹9.6 lakh in 20 years. Mistake: stopping early; the biggest gains come in later years.

**Emergency fund** — 6 months of essential expenses kept in a safe, easily withdrawn place (savings account, liquid fund, FD). Mistake: investing in shares money you may need next year.

**Risk vs return** — Higher possible returns come with higher chance of loss. Nothing gives high returns with no risk; anyone promising that is lying.

**Asset allocation** — How your money is split between equity (shares), debt (FD, bonds, PF), gold, etc. More important for most people than which stock they pick.

## Level 1 – How the market works

**Share / stock / equity** — A small piece of ownership in a company. Analogy: owning 1 brick of a building that earns rent. Mistake: treating a share as a lottery ticket instead of part of a business.

**Stock exchange (NSE, BSE)** — The regulated marketplace where shares are bought and sold. Like a mandi for shares, with strict rules.

**SEBI** — Securities and Exchange Board of India, the market regulator. Like a referee. Where: sebi.gov.in; check any adviser's registration there.

**Demat account** — Holds your shares electronically, like a bank account holds money. Held with a depository (NSDL or CDSL) through a broker.

**Trading account / broker** — Used to place buy and sell orders (Zerodha, Groww, Upstox, banks' broking arms, etc.).

**Market price vs value** — Price is what you pay today; value is what the business is actually worth. They can differ for a long time.

**Market capitalisation (market cap)** — Share price × number of shares = what the market says the whole company is worth. Large / mid / small cap (see framework).

**Order types: market and limit** — Market order buys at whatever the current price is; limit order buys only at your price or better. Mistake: market orders in illiquid small stocks, paying far more than expected.

**Settlement (T+1)** — Shares or money reach your account one working day after the trade.

**Upper / lower circuit** — The maximum a stock's price may move in a day; trading pauses or stops beyond it. Common in small, speculative stocks, a warning sign of thin trading.

**Bull and bear market** — Long periods of rising (bull) or falling (bear) prices.

## Level 2 – Mutual funds and indices

**Index (Nifty 50, Sensex)** — A basket representing the market. Nifty 50 = 50 large NSE companies; Sensex = 30 large BSE companies. "Market went up" usually means these went up.

**Mutual fund** — A pool of money from many investors managed by a professional fund manager. Analogy: a shared tiffin service; one cook, many customers, everyone gets a share.

**Index fund / ETF** — Funds that simply copy an index. Low cost, no fund-manager guesswork. Often the best starting point for beginners.

**SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)** — Investing a fixed amount every month automatically. Analogy: a monthly RD, but into a mutual fund. Benefit: you buy more units when prices are low (rupee cost averaging) and build the habit. Mistake: stopping SIP when the market falls, which is exactly when it buys cheapest.

**NAV** — Price of one unit of a mutual fund. A low NAV does not mean "cheap".

**Expense ratio** — Annual fee charged by the fund, as a % of your money. Small differences compound into big amounts over 20 years. Direct plans cost less than regular plans.

**Exit load** — Charge for withdrawing early (commonly within 1 year for equity funds).

## Level 3 – Reading a company

**Revenue (sales)** — Total money from selling goods/services. Prereq: none.

**Net profit (PAT)** — What remains after all costs, interest and tax.

**EPS** — Net profit ÷ number of shares = profit per share.

**P/E ratio** — Price ÷ EPS: how many rupees investors pay today for every ₹1 of the company's yearly profit per share. Analogy: a flat costing ₹50 lakh that earns ₹2 lakh rent a year is priced at 25 times its yearly rent. Don't describe P/E as "years to earn your money back": the company keeps most of its profit rather than paying it to you, and profit changes over time. Mistake: buying because P/E is low without asking why, or comparing with a broad sector average instead of close peers.

**ROE** — Profit ÷ shareholders' money. Analogy: shop owner put in ₹10 lakh, earns ₹2 lakh a year → ROE 20%.

**ROCE** — Operating profit ÷ (owners' money + borrowed money). Quality of the whole business.

**Debt-to-equity** — Borrowings ÷ owners' money. Family EMI analogy.

**Operating cash flow** — Actual cash generated by the business. Profit on paper should turn into cash.

**Capex (capital expenditure)** — Money spent on long-lasting assets: factories, machines, buildings. Analogy: a shopkeeper buying a bigger fridge or a second shop. Not an expense in the profit statement all at once, but real cash going out.

**Free cash flow (FCF)** — Operating cash flow minus capex: the cash left after keeping the business running and growing. Analogy: salary left after the EMI on a house extension. Mistake: treating negative FCF as automatically bad; growing companies often invest heavily for a few years. Ask how it's funded.

**Dividend** — Part of profit paid to shareholders in cash. Dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ price. Mistake: chasing high yield; a falling price can inflate yield.

**Book value / P/B** — Net worth per share; price ÷ book value. Important for banks.

**Promoter, promoter holding, pledge** — The founders/owning group, their % stake, and shares they've borrowed against.

**FII / DII** — Foreign and domestic institutional investors (big funds). Their buying/selling moves prices short-term.

**Annual report** — The company's yearly official document: business review, financial statements, auditor's report. The primary source.

**Quarterly results** — Financials every 3 months. Mistake: reacting to one quarter.

**Bonus issue / stock split** — More shares, same total value. Not free money; the price adjusts.

**Buyback** — Company buys back its own shares, usually signalling confidence and returning cash.

**IPO** — A company's first sale of shares to the public. Mistake: applying only for "listing gains".

## Level 4 – Risk and behaviour

**Diversification** — Not putting all eggs in one basket: several companies and sectors, or a fund. Mistake: owning 30 stocks all in one sector.

**Volatility** — How sharply prices swing. Normal for equity; a 10–20% fall happens in most years; a 30%+ fall happens every several years.

**Time horizon** — How long until you need the money. Equity suits 5+ years; not money needed soon.

**Stop-loss** — An order to sell if price falls to a set level, used by traders to cap losses.

**Averaging down** — Buying more as a price falls. Fine for a strong business you understand; dangerous for a weak one ("throwing good money after bad").

**FOMO and herd behaviour** — Buying because everyone else is. The most common way beginners buy near the top.

**Value trap** — A stock that looks cheap but keeps getting cheaper because the business is weakening.

**Penny stocks** — Very low-priced, usually tiny, thinly traded companies. A favourite tool of pump-and-dump scams.

## Level 5 – Understand, then usually avoid as a beginner

**Intraday trading** — Buying and selling within the same day. SEBI's own study found most individual intraday traders lose money (see market basics).

**Futures & Options (F&O / derivatives)** — Contracts on a share or index that expire, often with leverage (you control more than you pay). SEBI found about 9 in 10 individual F&O traders lost money. Teach what they are, so people aren't fooled, and be honest about the odds.

**Leverage / margin** — Trading with borrowed money. Magnifies both gains and losses; losses can exceed what you put in.

**Short selling** — Selling first, hoping to buy back cheaper. Loss is theoretically unlimited.
